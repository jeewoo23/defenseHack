\begin{abstract}
AERIS studies autonomous UAV role allocation for resilient ISR (Intelligence,
Surveillance, and Reconnaissance). The project couples mesh networking and
multi-modal UAV control: each UAV switches between ISR, mobile relay, and
static relay modes. Static relays trade mobility for higher effective bandwidth,
while ISR and mobile-relay UAVs preserve tactical flexibility. Since the
proposal, the simulator has matured into a benchmarkable graph testbed with
connected ISR scoring, adaptive relay branching, dual-objective threats,
cooperative observation, and persistent-surveillance area coverage. This
report describes the final controller: a \textbf{finite-horizon
(receding-horizon) optimizer} that evaluates a discrete set of seven
candidate policies via short-horizon rollouts and selects the maximizer of a
multi-objective mission score. The optimizer is compared against three
greedy variants (branching, emergency intercept, forward objective defense)
on a four-scenario UAV-vs-enemy sweep at 20 seeds per cell, and wins final
objective on all three hard scenarios.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The project addresses a core problem in contested ISR: surveillance is valuable
only if observations reach the operator. UAVs must decide when to sense, relay,
hold, or return to base while enemies move and links change. AERIS frames this
as online optimization over roles, relay placement, coverage, energy, and risk.

\section{Project Goal and Optimization Problem}
The original AERIS objective is to coordinate UAVs that switch between ISR,
mobile relay, and static relay modes. ISR observations have mission value only
when the UAV has a path back to the base, so sensing and connectivity must be
optimized jointly:
\begin{equation}
\max \; w_1 C_{\text{ISR}} + w_2 C_{\text{conn}}
- w_3 E - w_4 W_{\text{op}} - w_5 N_{\text{switch}}.
\label{eq:original_obj}
\end{equation}
Here $C_{\text{ISR}}$ is connected ISR coverage, $C_{\text{conn}}$ is
connectivity, $E$ is energy use, $W_{\text{op}}$ is operator burden, and
$N_{\text{switch}}$ penalizes role changes. The full problem is a stochastic
finite-horizon optimal control problem on a discrete-time hybrid system
(continuous UAV positions plus discrete role variables), which is intractable
to solve exactly.

\section{Simulator}
The simulator implements a discrete-time UAV team. UAVs move, consume battery,
return to base, recharge, and switch among ISR, mobile relay, and static relay
modes. Each timestep rebuilds the communication graph; ISR observations count
only if the observing UAV is connected to the base through the graph. The
evaluation layer tracks time-weighted coverage, detection latency, flank
coverage, UAV losses, strikes, connectivity, and objective health. A
dual-objective scenario with undefended north and south sites forces multi-axis
behavior, and a benchmark runner evaluates policies over multiple random seeds.

\section{Greedy Baseline}
The starting controller is a greedy heuristic. At each timestep it allocates a
relay budget, clusters enemies, assigns relay waypoints, promotes relays when
needed, and assigns connected ISR UAVs to nearby or urgent enemies via
band-aware round-robin matching. It is useful as a proof of concept --
autonomous role allocation and relay-supported ISR work in simulation -- but
the policy is local and reactive. It does not compare future action sequences,
solve a constrained assignment problem, or explicitly optimize against
predicted objective damage.

\section{Finite-Horizon Optimizer}
The final controller is a \textbf{receding-horizon (model-predictive) controller
operating on a discrete candidate policy set} -- a one-step \textit{rollout
algorithm} in the sense of Bertsekas. At each replan point the controller
generates a small set of high-level candidate policies, simulates each forward
$H$ steps in a cloned copy of the world, scores the terminal state with a
multi-objective $J$, executes the maximizing candidate for $K$ steps, and
replans.

\subsection{Multi-Objective Score}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
J = &\;\alpha C_{\text{ISR}} + \beta C_{\text{conn}}
+ \gamma H_{\text{obj}} + \eta S_{\text{strike}} + \nu C_{\text{area}} \\
&- \lambda D_{\text{obj}} - \mu L_{\text{detect}}
- \rho L_{\text{UAV}} - \psi E_{\text{battery}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:horizon_obj}
\end{equation}
$H_{\text{obj}}$ is mean surviving objective health, $D_{\text{obj}}$ is total
objective damage suffered during the rollout, $S_{\text{strike}}$ is strikes
completed, $L_{\text{detect}}$ is detection latency, $L_{\text{UAV}}$ is UAV
losses, $E_{\text{battery}}$ is mean battery drain, and $C_{\text{area}}$ is
the persistent-surveillance area-coverage fraction (cells of a $25\times25$
grid observed within the last $50$ steps by a connected ISR sensor).
Weights are chosen so that a full loss of both objectives outweighs any
other term, making the optimizer act decisively when sites are projected to
fall.

\subsection{Cooperative Observation Mechanic}
The strike mechanic was extended so that when $k$ connected ISRs simultaneously
observe the same enemy, the consecutive-observation counter advances by
$\min(k, 2)$ per step. The cap at 2 reflects fire-mission doctrine:
redundant sensors accelerate the kill chain, but past two the artillery
response time -- not the sensor count -- becomes the bottleneck. Combined
with a reduced strike window ($20\to12$ steps) this makes outnumbered
scenarios meaningfully winnable.

\subsection{Candidate Set}
At each replan the controller scores seven candidate policies: (i) branching
chain only, (ii) branching with emergency intercept of objective-bound enemies,
(iii) intercept without branching, (iv) single-chain (no branching, no
intercept), (v) persistent objective-lane watch, (vi) forward objective
defense with branching (a per-objective mobile relay chain ending in a
forward ISR observation post on the enemy approach axis), and
(vii) objective defense combined with intercept. Each candidate is a parameter
override applied to the existing greedy policy, not a separate controller --
this keeps the implementation compact while letting the optimizer pick the
right autonomy layer for the current state.

\subsection{Algorithm}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every $K=5$ steps, snapshot the simulation and deepcopy it.
\item For each candidate policy $\pi$, run the clone for $H=20$ steps under
      $\pi$ and evaluate $J$ on the terminal state. $H$ is chosen to span
      the 12-step strike-completion window with margin so that candidates
      initiating a strike see it complete inside the rollout.
\item Select $\pi^{\star} = \arg\max_\pi J(\pi)$.
\item Execute $\pi^{\star}$ on the real simulator for the next $K$ steps.
\end{enumerate}
This restricts the action space from an infinite-dimensional control space to a
finite argmax, while the rollout supplies an approximation of the cost-to-go
that the full Bellman equation would otherwise require.

\subsection{Emergency Intercept}
The emergency intercept behavior (used by candidates (ii) and (iii)) claims
the nearest connected ISR for any objective-bound enemy whose time-to-objective
falls below a threshold, bypassing the normal band-aware matching. This
addresses the original failure mode in which objective-bound enemies were not
detected early enough to support a strike before the site took damage.

\section{Experimental Setup and Results}
Each controller was evaluated over 500-timestep runs across 20 random seeds
on four dual-objective scenarios that vary the friend-to-foe ratio:
outnumbered (10 UAVs vs 15 enemies), even (12 vs 12), current
(14 vs 9, the scenario used in earlier sections), and abundant (18 vs 6).
The four controllers compared are greedy with branching, greedy with
emergency intercept, greedy with forward objective defense, and the
finite-horizon optimizer.

\section{Discussion}
The scenario sweep, after layering cooperative observation and a reduced
strike window onto the v3 scenario tuning, yields the strongest evidence
for the finite-horizon controller in this report.

First, \textbf{horizon wins final objective on three of four scenarios},
including all three of the hardest. On 10v15 outnumbered it scores
$-10.47$ vs defense $-12.37$ and greedy $-15.41$; on 12v12 and 14v9 it
takes the lead by similar margins. On 18v6 abundant all four controllers
finish near $0$ (within noise) and greedy edges horizon, $0.84$ vs
$-0.38$, because the simple heuristic is already near-optimal when the
problem is easy. The pattern is consistent across the report: horizon is
worth the most exactly when it's needed most.

Second, \textbf{the second-best controller varies across scenarios},
which is the core justification for the discrete candidate-set design.
Defense is second-best on 10v15 (where its extra defensive structure pays
off) and the best site-preserver on 18v6 (where it has the UAV budget).
Greedy is second on 12v12 and 14v9. Intercept is rarely the right choice
but occasionally clears a site at high variance. No single hand-tuned
policy dominates across scenarios; horizon's rollouts recover the right
choice per state without any per-scenario hand tuning.

Third, \textbf{the two mechanism changes -- cooperative observation and
the 12-step strike window -- compound to make outnumbered scenarios
meaningfully winnable for the first time}. Compared to the earlier sweep
(20-step strike window, no coop-obs), every controller improved by
$+3$ to $+7$ on final objective in every scenario. Mean site preservation
on the hardest scenario jumped from 5--20\% to 22--32\% across
controllers. The previously catastrophic standalone defense policy now
scores second-best on 10v15 and best on 18v6 -- the diversity in
horizon's candidate set finally has a scenario where every candidate
contributes.

Fourth, area coverage values remain similar across controllers
($\approx 0.08$--$0.16$). The new $\nu C_{\text{area}}$ term in $J$ adds a
near-uniform baseline rather than discriminating between candidates, but
it does suppress degenerate cluster-on-base behavior that would otherwise
emerge once an enemy is struck. Under all four scenarios, optimization
remains dominated by enemy engagement, not by where UAVs would otherwise
loiter.

\section{Conclusion}
AERIS now spans both ends of the optimization spectrum: a fast greedy
heuristic that handles the steady state, and a receding-horizon optimizer that
evaluates a discrete candidate set against a multi-objective score before
acting. The reframing is what matters: the controller is no longer one
hand-coded policy but a selector over policies, scored by predicted mission
outcome. The final 20-seed scenario sweep shows that the finite-horizon controller
is the best-scoring policy on all three hard scenarios (10v15, 12v12,
14v9) and finishes within noise of greedy on the easiest (18v6). The
second-best controller varies across scenarios -- defense on 10v15 and
18v6, greedy on 12v12 and 14v9 -- which is the strongest possible
justification for horizon's discrete candidate-set design. Cooperative
observation (cap 2) combined with a 12-step strike window made every
controller substantially more effective, with the largest absolute lifts
landing on the previously broken outnumbered scenario. Future work
includes upgrading within-candidate UAV-to-enemy matching from greedy to
Hungarian assignment, learning the $J$ weights from offline rollout data,
sweeping the area-coverage weight $\nu$ to find a discriminative operating
point, and pursuing dual-ISR forward defense to push site preservation
above 50\% on the hardest scenario.
